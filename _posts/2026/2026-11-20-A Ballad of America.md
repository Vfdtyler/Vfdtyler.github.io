title: A Ballad of America

The Grand Theft Auto series is a reflection of America. But it's not an idealized or romanticized depiction. GTA presents America as it is, not as we would like it to be. Everything that is beautiful, ugly, ridiculous, and deeply sad is reflected back at us. And that reflection is much more honest than a lot of other mainstream media.

Grand Theft Auto VI was released yesterday, 13 years after the previous game. America has changed a lot in 13 years, and now it is once again time for us to look at ourselves in the mirror of GTA and see what we've become.

The New York Times Magazine published [a very good piece][nyt] about the game and I recommend reading the whole thing, but here's my favorite part:
> I like to think of Grand Theft Auto as two different but complementary experiences. The first is more or less what its critics accuse it of being: a hunk of nasty pulp. The second — much less appreciated — is a satire that pulls the nastiness into quotation marks. If most violent video games are grim and airless, the Grand Theft Auto series is in the madcap tradition of Looney Tunes: dumb done smart, lousy with references high, low and in between. Importantly, the experiences don’t work without each other. In the absence of the satire, the playable mayhem would be sort of indefensible. In the absence of the playable mayhem, the satire would find its way to no one.

But the games are not just satire, they're also stories about the American dream. What does the American dream look like today? Is that dream even attainable? What if the only way to achieve this dream is to go outside of the law? And what do those answers say about us?

There is a long tradition of using crime drama to tell a story about America. You can draw a straight line from The Great Train Robbery (1903) to Shane (1953) to The Godfather (1972) to Grand Theft Auto VI (2026). Like those that came before it, Grand Theft Auto is a great American epic. It is a ballad of America.
